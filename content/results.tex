\section{Analysis and Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section presents the analysis of the results of the user study presented in 
\fref{sec:evaluation}. The user study was conducted on a single session with 27 participants.
The results are presented according to the three sections in the survey: General 
knowledge (\fref{sec:general-knowledge}), Task Effectiveness (\fref{sec:tasks-results}), and Usability 
(\fref{sec:usability}). Afterwards, we use the information extracted from the responses analysis to 
answer our three research questions. Finally, this section closes with a discussion of the main take 
aways that can be taken from the user study in perspective of the research questions, to then discuss 
the threats to validity of the study. 

All data obtained from the questionnaire is available in our 
online appendix (\url{https://flaglab.github.io/Flik_Experiments/}).

\subsubsection{General knowledge questions results}
\label{sec:general-knowledge}

The demographic questions of the questionnaire are used to assess the population of participants, 
shown in \fref{fig:general-know}. Participants are graduate students, from the engineering school, 
with at least one year of experience in programming, having taken the mandatory courses for the 
master program, given that the \ac{RL} course they have taken is an advanced course in the program. 

Most participants report to be very experienced with Python 
(\fref{fig:python-experience}) and using the terminal (\fref{fig:terminal-experience}). In both categories 
the participants report to have used Python often in their work and/or university courses (for at least 
4 years during the bachelor). 
The reported experience in \ac{RL} is more spread out, with a tendency towards familiarity with
\ac{RL} (\fref{fig:rl-experience}). The spread in the experience level about using \ac{RL} may be due 
to the lack of confidence about the expertise, as many of the participants have taken a course in 
\ac{RL} but may feel that they may need further experience.
However, the familiarity reported about the experience in using debuggers is less prominent with an 
average lack of familiarity in debugger use. Upon revision of the comments about the experience 
using debuggers, many (14) participants (all reporting a level of experience with Python between 4 
and 5) reported rare to no usage of debuggers. While some of the answers (4 participants) hint at the 
use of the debugger incorporated with \acp{IDE} like PyCharm and VSCode, in most cases the 
participants (10) report not to use debugging facilities at all, and prefer the use of print statements.  
The participants that do report experience using debuggers (14), use a variety of debuggers like 
PyCharm, PDB, or VSCode in their daily work activities.

\subsubsection{Tasks questions results}
\label{sec:tasks-results}

\fref{fig:solved-tasks} summarizes the results of the information reported by the participants in 
solving the tasks. The y-axis represents the number of participants for each answer category at the  
given Likert score. The x-axis corresponds to the score, with values in the range from 5 to 1 (from 
completely agree, to completely disagree). Each of the bars in the graph represents a question for 
the tasks (three in total). The third question (the third bar in green for all graphs), is used as additional 
information to check the coherence of time completion, given an impression of the time taken to solve 
each of the tasks, where 5 represents taking a lot of time in completing the task (taking the full 30 minutes or not completing the task), and 1 represents taking little time (under 6 minutes).

Taking the results reported from the three tasks, most of the participants completed the first task 
successfully (\fref{fig:task1}). Participants reported the task was easy to solve. Even though the task 
was reported to be easy, it took participants a long time to complete, because this was the task in 
which participants had to get familiarized with the tool. 

The second task was harder than the first one, as participants were not familiar with the task to 
solve, the Rooms environment, and it is of a higher complexity than the first environment. Most of 
the participants took over half the time given to solve task (\fref{fig:task2}). Examining the 
participants that took the longest to solve the task, we observe that they did not find the bug 
successfully (2 out of 4). In contrast from the participants taking the least amount of time (1 and 2 in 
the Likert scale) very few (only 2 out of 9) did not successfully find the bug. Such correlation between 
the increase in time to solve the task and the decrease in the correct solutions to the task can be 
explained from two perspectives. First, the bug introduced in task 2, does not exhibit a completely 
erroneous agent behavior, but a more subtle interaction with the environment and the obtained 
Q-values. Second, fixing the bug involves modifying the learning function, which, based on the 
comments about the task confused some of the participants. 

Finally, the third task (\fref{fig:task3}) was the most challenging task. This is confirmed by the amount 
of participants who completed the task effectively, only 6 of the 8 participants that reported solving the 
task (5 in the Likert score) effectively solved it based on their answer to the bug identification. Moreover, 
from those participants we observe that the time taken to solve the task was significant (Likert score 
of 4) for 5 of the participants. Most participants had trouble finding the bug. In contrast, out of the 10 
participants that reported completing the task promptly (Likert scores of 1 and 2), 8 effectively solved 
it. 6 participants did not detect the bug.

While the third task was not easy to solve according to participants' comments, they were effective in 
solving it. From the results it is expected that identifying and fixing the bug in the first task took longer 
than for other tasks, even though this is the easiest task, as participants were still getting familiarized 
with \flik, and therefore explored the capabilities of \flik more, while detecting the bug. Such behavior 
points to a steeper learning curve for debugging the programs with \flik. In the following tasks, while the 
nature of the introduced bugs is different, the expertise gained using the tool was beneficial in 
detecting the bugs. For the second task, given the nature of the bug and the fact that the bug was 
introduced in the Q-learning algorithm's logic, made it a harder bug to identify; even though it was 
completed by most participants. For the third task, we observe that the time to solve the task starts 
to decrease with reported times leaning towards shorter times to solve the task.

\subsection{Answers to the research questions}
\label{sec:answers}

Based on the results from the user study, we now turn our attention to the answering the research 
questions.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ.\arabic*}]
  \item Refers to the appropriateness of a dedicated debugger for developing and debugging \ac{RL} 
  programs. To answer this \textbf{RQ} we use the experience in using debuggers, reported by 
  participants, and the comments provided at the end of the questionnaire. 
  
  From the answers, 59\% of the participants found the tool useful and would recommend its use for 
  the case of \ac{RL}. 
  In the cases in which they would not recommend it, some clarify that they would not recommend it 
  as is, pointing to improvements in the interaction with the tool and the lack of a GUI. Taking into 
  account that 51\% of the users report not having experience with debuggers or not using them 
  regularly, they still found \flik to be of use to debug \ac{RL} programs. Furthermore, when asked 
  participants if they think \flik would have been useful for the development of their \ac{RL} course, a 
  great majority (89\%) found it would have helped their experience. 
  
  Given the answers collected from the participants, we can state \flik is useful for debugging \ac{RL} 
  programs. 
  
  \item Refers specifically to the features developed in \flik and their usefulness to detect bugs. To 
  answer this question \textbf{RQ} we use answers given by the participants about the strategy to 
  solve the bug, and the comments given at the end of the questionnaire.
  
  The answers given by the users for each task do not evidence any relevance or pertinence with 
  respect to the use or usefulness of the features developed in \flik for detecting bug. The comments 
  present seldom information about the pertinence of the features. Only two comments from 
  participants hint to the usefulness of the features, stating that the forward and backward navigation 
  provided by \flik is useful, but it would be more useful if the navigations would be supported by a GUI 
  or if the traces of execution would be recorded.
  
  Given the answers collected from the participants we cannot assert the usefulness of the features 
  presented in \flik to debug \ac{RL} programs, but some of the users identify their potential.
  
  \item Refers to the effectiveness of using \flik to debug \ac{RL} programs. To answer this \textbf{RQ} 
  we use the questions of time completeness and the easiness to complete the task, cross-checked 
  with the perception of the time to complete each task, and the answers to the questions about the 
  detected bug, and the explanation about the fix of the bug.
  
  With respect to the effectiveness, we 
  can observe that study participants, for the most part, were successful in using \flik to detect the 
  bug in each of the tasks, with a success rate of 88\%, 85\%, and 55\% for each of the tasks 
  respectively.
  
  Together with the effectiveness to solve the bugs, we observe a tendency among the participants who 
  to take a short time in detecting the bug. In average, the perception of the time taken to solve a task 
  moves from a Likert score of 4 and 5 in the first task, to a score of 2 and 3 in the last task. Such 
  tendency suggests that, once participants are used to using \flik, they can more effectively identify 
  more involved bugs within the code.
  
  Taking into account the results and answers given by the participants, we can state that \flik is 
  indeed effective in helping developers detect bugs in a timely manner.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

In general, the results of the user study evaluation are very positive. Most of the participants have a 
good impression of \flik, finding it to be useful, especially for the kind of challenges presented in 
\ac{RL} programs. The participants found it hard to get familiarize with the tool, especially 
for the participants with little experience with debuggers, which would deter the use of the debugger. 
Nonetheless, participants found the tool useful in over 88\% of the cases. At the beginning of the 
evaluation session, most of the participants found the tool easy to use (after some initial use). 
Additionally, participants mentioned the tool be of use in general for the development of \ac{RL} 
programs, for example, to develop course work, identifying the errors in the study as recurrent 
during development. The participants to the user study also highlight that \textbf{\flik is useful in 
detecting common and hard to identify errors and misbehavior in \ac{RL} programs}; reinforcing the 
usefulness of an \ac{RL} specific debugger.

While there is no evidence about the usefulness of the features developed specifically for \flik, these 
features are used in detecting the bugs by most participants. Additionally, the mastery of the tool as 
tasks progress, can corelate to the decrease in the time to solve the tasks. Detecting the bugs faster 
hints to a better use of the tool, and in particular, a better use of its features. Nonetheless, given that 
there was no control group to detect bugs in the programs using a standard Python debugger or no 
debugger at all, at this time we cannot assure the usefulness of the features on their own. This work 
is left as future work for the evaluation with larger groups of participants.

The effectiveness and efficiency of using \flik is demonstrated by the relation between the tasks 
effectively solved, and the time taken to solve the tasks. These two variables highlight an 
improvement in solving the tasks more efficiently as user gain more experience with the tool. While 
the number of effective detections to the bug decrease as the tasks progress, which in the latter 
cases can be attributed to the increased complexity and environment space of the tasks, the 
resolution rate is over 50\% in all cases. Such results make a case for further development of 
specialized tools, and in particular \flik, to support the development of \ac{RL} programs.
 
With respect to the usability dimension the participants reported that, with some difficulties at the 
beginning, again for the participants without previous experience with debuggers, 
\textbf{\flik is usable}. The usability of the tool is confirmed by the completion of the three tasks by a 
majority of the participants, and the rapid learning curve gathered when switching between tasks, 
reaching a faster and more fluent code navigation to inspect the program, identify the cause of the 
error, and fix the bug, as reported in the time taken to complete each task. Nonetheless, the 
participants suggested that to increase the usability of \flik \textit{it is desirable to implement more GUI 
features closer to common features used in other debuggers, as the Visual Studio Code debugger}.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

To conclude the discussion of the results and the experiment we present the threats to validity to 
our experimentation and the conclusions extracted from it.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.1cm, labelindent=0.1cm]
\item[Threats to external validity] refer to the generalization of the results from our evaluation. 
\flik is designed as a debugger for \ac{RL} programs enabling developers to inspect the internal state 
of their agents and the environment state while they execute. In doing so, developers can change the 
values of variables, and observe agent behavior from any point in the execution. Our evaluation and 
user study explores the usability of \flik in the case of discrete agents based on the Q-learning 
algorithm. From the results, we confirm \flik's usability in such cases, and can generalize the results 
to other discrete algorithms such as SARSA or Multi-armed-bandit~\cite{lattimore20}. However, 
generalizing the usability to continuous \ac{RL} environments requires further evaluation in such 
environments, in which the intervention of developers with the system and the possibility to 
observe all environments variables and parameters may be limited due to the sheer volume of 
variables, and the use of external \acl{DNN} libraries \flik may not have access to.

A bias towards the generalization of the results exists in the selection of the participants in the user 
study. The participants have very similar backgrounds and previously know the evaluators (discussed 
in the next threat). As such the perceptions about the tool could be biased to the population. We 
highlight that a more extensive evaluation with a wider range of participants could strengthen the 
early conclusion obtained from our evaluation.

\item[Threats to construct validity] refer to the cases in which the evaluation is not exhaustive 
or selective enough. Our validation is built on the user study of \flik. In the user study we worked
with users that had at least some basic experience with \ac{RL}. From the open call to the 
evaluation, the user study had 27 participants. While the participants are all students, they do have
diverse profiles, maturity level, and experience in working with \ac{RL}. Therefore, we can state that 
there is no bias in the selection of the participants for the user study. Some of the participants were 
familiar with the authors, either by taking courses with them or as colleagues during courses. We 
note there could be a bias to favor the outcome of the evaluation of the tool given the familiar 
relation. Nonetheless, not all the participants had the bias, balancing out its effect. This can be 
observed from the comments given by participants of constructive improvements for the tool, or 
stating their dislike for the tool. Moreover, this activity was independent from any academic activity, 
striving to mitigate the bias.

A learning bias could affect the results of the tasks as the user study progressed. To this effect, the  
user study was designed such that the first exercise is familiar to all developers 
with \ac{RL} experience, looking to help participants familiarize themselves with \flik (after the 
presentation of \flik in a small example). The following tasks in the study are of less familiarity for 
\ac{RL} developers, and the last is a completely new application, mitigating the effects of any 
previous knowledge developers may have about possible errors in the application. 

There is no bias about the application selection favoring the usability of \flik over other applications. 
Neither there is a bias in the bugs inserted in each of the applications, as the nature of the bugs 
changes for each of the applications, covering common cases of bugs in \ac{RL} programs. We 
highlight again the need to evaluate \flik in continuous environments.

\item[Threats to internal validity] refers to the conditions that may have an impact on the 
phenomena under test. The user study was set so that all participants could execute under the 
same conditions: characteristics of the machines in which the evaluation was executed, information 
and documentation of \flik, access to \flik experts, and time constraints to work on the different 
applications, reducing any bias that may have appeared in the execution of the user study 
compromising the results.

Regarding the length of the user study, which may affect the usefulness of feedback given by the 
participants at the end of the questionnaire. The session was dynamic and the users demonstrated 
active interest about the tool and solving the tasks, so that tiredness was not a factor. While the risk was 
not foreseen, it was self-mitigated, which can be confirmed by the amount of comments received (all 
participants added comments), many of which were on point, and serve as a guide for future 
improvements about the tool. Related to this risk we identified the time constraints to complete tasks 
may affect the completion of task by different users. However, as efficiency or productivity are not 
variables of interest to this study, participants that did not complete a task were told to leave the task 
behind and continue into the next one, mitigating the effect of completing the task on a given time may 
cause.
\end{description}
